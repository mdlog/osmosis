The last night train still runs on time

A demo article written for Osmosis · 4 min read

Every evening at a quarter past ten, a small blue train leaves the station at the edge of the old town. It has only four cars, and most nights fewer than thirty people get on. The ticket costs less than a cup of coffee, and the trip to the coast takes almost six hours.

People ask the driver why the line still exists. She laughs and says that the train is slow on purpose. There is no wifi on board, the windows open, and the cafe car sells soup, bread and very strong tea. For many passengers, the night train is the only quiet place they have all week.

Marta has worked on the line for twenty years. She knows the regular passengers by name and remembers which stop each of them needs. A teacher travels every Friday to visit her mother. A young musician carries his guitar to small concerts by the sea. An old man rides the whole way, has breakfast on the beach, and comes back the next night.

Last spring the city wanted to close the line because it lost money every month. Hundreds of people wrote letters, and children painted pictures of the blue train for the council. In the end, the city decided to keep it for at least five more years. Some of the letters now hang on the wall of the cafe car.

The train does not try to be fast or modern. It stops in villages where nobody seems to live, and it waits when a farmer is late. Passengers read, sleep, play cards, or simply look at the dark fields. When the sun comes up, the sea appears on the right side, and everyone moves to the windows at the same time.

Marta says that the best part of her job is that moment. Strangers who did not speak all night suddenly smile at each other. Then the train arrives, the doors open, and the cold air smells of salt. She drinks one last cup of tea, and the blue train gets ready to go home.
